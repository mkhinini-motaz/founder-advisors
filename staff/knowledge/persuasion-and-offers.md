# Offers, persuasion, negotiation and people

Craft borrowed from other fields, filtered for an honest small business. Every point rests on free public material: the author's own talks on YouTube, free articles and papers, or a book in the public domain. Each section names its source, opened on 21 Nov 2026. Unlike the country files, nothing here expires, but everything here can be misused: read the filter first.

## The filter

Use a mechanism only if the customer, supplier or partner would thank you after seeing exactly how it works. Real scarcity, real deadlines, real proof, real bonuses. The brief's honesty rules and the law (no fake urgency, no hidden fees, no pre-ticked consent) override every tactic below. When a tactic and the filter disagree, the filter wins and the tactic is dropped.

## Hormozi on offers (talks, 2020s)

Sources: his talk "This ONE Equation Will Make You RICH" [1], his talk "This video will make you more money than anything else on the Internet" [2] and "The Offer Is King (ALEX HORMOZI)" [3]. His free course page at acquisition.com lists the lesson titles (market, pricing, value equation, bonuses, guarantees, scarcity and urgency, naming) but carries no text, so nothing here rests on it [4].

- **Value equation.** Four variables. Top: the dream outcome and the perceived likelihood of achieving it. Bottom: the time delay and the effort and sacrifice. Raise the top, cut the bottom [1]. Our line: a customer, supplier or partner reads each of the four in our offer, so check each.
- **Bottom is harder to copy.** He says big promises and testimonials are easy; faster results and less effort need real operations, and that is where the lasting edge sits (his examples: Netflix, Amazon) [1].
- **Early win.** Deliver some result first, even a small one, before the full result. His example: a seven-day mini book boot camp ahead of the full book [1].
- **Do not be the commodity.** If buyers see two offers as the same, they decide on price. Lowering price makes that worse; compete on value [2].
- **Trim and stack.** List every problem the customer meets, in order, with a solution for each. Rank each by value to the customer and by cost to you. Keep the high-value, low-cost ones and cut the rest [1].
- **Sell against inaction.** The real competitor is doubt and the comfort of not buying. Cut risk and friction so acting is easy [1].
- **Guarantees** [1] [5]. Four types: unconditional (the strongest), conditional (if the buyer does X, we do Y), anti-guarantee (say plainly that sales are final) and implied (pay on performance). Spell the promise out in plain words with the specific result and date, not "results guaranteed". Keep conditions few, or the guarantee weakens. His argument: extra sales can outweigh extra refunds, so check the numbers before refusing a guarantee. Our line: an anti-guarantee needs a true reason (a limited drop, a secret that cannot be un-learned); an invented reason fails the filter.
- **Believable offers** [3]. Give a reason why, such as being overstocked and making room for new stock. Make the value clear in a few words (he uses "delivered to your door in 30 minutes or it's free"). An offer too good to believe gets no replies. He says most businesses should rest on one exceptional core offer.
- **Enhancers.** He lists five ways to add value to an offer: scarcity, urgency, bonuses, guarantees and a name that fits the buyer [1] [2]. Scarcity example: a luxury brand that stocks one or two pieces per store keeps its value [6]. Our line: scarcity and urgency are allowed only when the cap or deadline is real.

## Hormozi on leads (talks)

Sources: his live launch talk for the book [7] and the short talk "Give Away Free Stuff" [8].

- **The core four.** Four ways to tell people about your offer: warm outreach (one to one, people who know you), free content, paid ads and cold outreach [7]. Start with the warm one you can do today. Our line: paid ads come last, when an offer already converts warm traffic.
- **Lead magnets.** A complete solution to a narrow problem, given free before the paid thing, so more people buy [7]. Free work that costs you real money is valued more than a PDF [8]. For a supplier: free setup work they would otherwise do themselves. For customers: a first-order offer that passes the CFO's rule (funder, cap, end date); that rule is ours.
- **Content:** the framework is hook, retain, reward [7].
- **Lead getters.** Customers, employees, agencies and affiliates advertise for you, using the core four [7]. Our line: ask a customer for a referral at the moment of peak happiness (the delivered order, the first payout) and reward both sides after the friend's first order.

## Hormozi on money models (talk)

Source: his live launch talk for the book [9], with the lesson titles on his free course page [10].

- **A money model is a sequence of offers.** His definition: a sequence of offers that make customers spend twice as much on you as you do on them in 30 days or less. Then cash stops limiting growth [9]. Written as a formula: 30-day gross profit above two times acquisition cost plus cost of goods [9].
- **Four mechanisms.** Attraction offers (get people to buy), upsell offers (spend more), downsell offers (buy something after a no) and continuity offers (buy repeatedly) [9]. The course page names examples: win your money back, free giveaways, decoy offers, buy X get Y; menu and anchor upsells; payment plans and free trials as downsells; continuity bonuses, discounts and waived fees [10].
- **His gym example.** A free trial that needs three trials per paying member and three months to break even, against a paid start with a "win your money back" goal and a follow-on offer. Same gym, better money model [9].
- **Marketplace translation (ours).** Attraction = the welcome offer on the first order, funded by a supplier where possible. Upsell = a bigger basket or a bundle at checkout. Continuity = points, a subscription pass, a supplier plan. Each still needs a funder, a cap and an end date.

## Persuasion psychology

- **Cialdini's principles** [11]. His organisation's free article states six: reciprocity, scarcity, authority, consistency, liking and social proof. Our applications: reciprocity (give first: the photos, the typed menu), consistency (a small first yes, then put it in writing), social proof (the neighbours who are live, local numbers), authority (shown competence, not titles), liking (real compliments, the same town), scarcity (real only).
- **Anchoring** [12]. Tversky and Kahneman (1974): people estimate from a starting value, adjust too little, and different starting values give different answers. Our applications: show the all-in total before its parts; open a negotiation with the written terms.
- **Choice overload** [13]. Iyengar and Lepper (2000): in a jam-tasting field study, 60% of passers-by stopped at the 24-jam display, yet 30% of those who saw 6 jams later bought, against 3% of those who saw 24. Our line: curate the list; offer "which of these two", not "anything you like".
- **Goal gradient** [14]. Kivetz, Urminsky and Zheng (2006): café customers with a 12-stamp card that came with two stamps already done finished the purchases faster than customers with a plain 10-stamp card. Our line: a loyalty card that starts with a head start beats an empty one, if the head start is disclosed.
- **Zero price** [15]. Shampanier and Ariely: when the cheaper product goes from a low price to free, far more people choose it and fewer choose the dearer one, with the price gap unchanged. Our line: expect part of the extra demand for "free delivery" to leave when the price returns, and do not read free sign-ups as willingness to pay.
- **Defaults** [16]. Johnson and Goldstein: the choice of default can change the outcome, shown by comparing countries with opt-in and opt-out organ donation. Our line: make the default what the person would choose knowingly; consent is never pre-ticked.

## Negotiation

- **Interests, not positions** [17]. Harvard's Program on Negotiation: positions are surface statements, interests are the needs underneath. Settle only on terms better than your BATNA ("The goal is to reach an agreement that is better than your BATNA"). Use fair, independent standards agreed in advance. Brainstorm options before committing. Our standards: the written terms, a competitor's published rate, the carrier's tariff.
- **Voss's techniques** [18]. The Black Swan Group, his firm, defines: a mirror repeats the last three to five words in an inquisitive tone; a label names an emotion or circumstance the other side has not voiced; calibrated questions start with what, how or sometimes why; an accusation audit raises the negative thoughts first; "that's right" comes when you summarise their point so well they can only agree. Our examples: label "it sounds like the last platform burned you"; audit "you may think we are like the others".
- **Our own rules.** Open with the written terms when you know the market; let the other side name a number first when they know more than you. The relationship outlives the deal in a small town, and a win that humiliates the other side costs later deals. These are judgment, not sourced.

## Advertising and positioning classics

- **Hopkins, *Scientific Advertising* (1923)** [19]. Public domain: a US work published before 1931 [20]. Test and trace: key each ad with a coupon, a separate number or a link, and judge by cost per customer, not replies alone. Be specific: his line is that a man who makes a specific claim "is either telling the truth or a lie". Superlatives like "best in the world" claim only the expected and can harm. Offer a sample or a trial where possible: he describes a free-week offer where about nine in ten trials led to sales. Our example: "delivered in 30 minutes, 3 euros" beats "fast and cheap". Caveat: he wrote for print and mail order; test the same rules on Facebook, Instagram and TikTok.
- **Ries and Trout on positioning** [21]. Their 1972 *Advertising Age* articles, free on the Ries site: a company must create a position in the prospect's mind; the market floods with "me-too" products; people name rarely more than seven brands in a category; competing head-on against the leader does not work. Our line: define a category you can lead ("the local one", "all-in, no surprises") rather than claiming to be a better version of the leader.
- **Owned, not rented** [22]. Lavingia's free skills repo, based on his book and published by him: social media is rented land (algorithms change, accounts can be shut down); an email list is owned. Our line: a phone number, a push token or an email with marketing consent is owned; count that list as the asset and turn followers into it. Unsure whether to count this source as free author material (see the report).

## Carnegie on speaking (*The Art of Public Speaking*, 1915, with Esenwein)

The book is free in full at Project Gutenberg [23]: Gutenberg says its books are public domain in the United States, and the work is from 1915. Carnegie's later *How to Win Friends and Influence People* (1936) is not free, so nothing here rests on it.

- **Confidence comes from facing audiences and knowing the subject:** face an audience as often as you can; the authors write "Know your subject better than your hearers know it, and you have nothing to fear." (ch. 1).
- **Monotony is the cardinal sin of the speaker** (ch. 2).
- **Specific words are stronger than general words** (the book's example: "pressman" over "printer").
- **For a counter pitch, a video script or a talk to your team:** one point, one story, one ask (ours).
