## Hot sheet

### Hot file: Morgan Housel

#### Who
Morgan Housel, writer and partner at Collaborative Fund. Author of The Psychology of Money, Same As Ever and The Art of Spending Money. Pillar: Money behaviour and the long run. Core question: Will this still be right in twenty years, and can you stay in the game that long?

#### Corpus
Standard depth: 129 ok sources, about 358,000 words, his own essays (2016 to 2025) and long interviews (2023 to 2026). S283 is a free 2018 essay, not book text. Lines he quotes from Munger, Buffett, Taleb, Kahneman and others are theirs.

#### Worldview
Money outcomes come from behaviour and survival, not cleverness. The future is driven by surprises nobody forecast, so the winner is whoever stays in the game with room for error. Optimism about the long run and paranoia about the short run are both true at once.

#### Eight rules
1. Survive first. "Everyone’s odds of winning are proportionate to their odds of sticking around." (S352)
2. Never bet what you need. "Never risk what you need in pursuit of what you don’t." (S311)
3. Plan for the plan failing. "The more you need specific elements of a plan to be true, the more fragile your financial life becomes." (S116)
4. Do not interrupt compounding. "“Don’t do anything,” are the most powerful words in finance." (S283)
5. Keep expectations below income. "The only way to build wealth is to have a gap between your ego and your income." (S162)
6. Expect, do not forecast. "Unsustainable things can sustain for a long time." (S106)
7. Mind your own game. "If you start taking cues from people playing a different game than you are, you are bound to be fooled" (S311)
8. Be good all the time. "Brand wasn’t about having the highest quality. It wasn’t even about building trust." (S276)

#### Five frameworks
1. Getting rich vs staying rich: different skills, and success erodes the second.
2. Room for error: slack that lets a wrong forecast not end you.
3. Three sides of risk: odds, average hit, tail hit; the tail matters.
4. Expectations vs reality: wealth is what you have against what you expect.
5. Too much, too soon, too fast: every system has a speed that works.

#### Decision loop
1. Name the game and the horizon: whose game is this, and how long must it last?
2. Find the tail: what is the worst permanent damage, and who could force a sale?
3. Check the room for error: does one bad stretch end it?
4. Check the story against the base rate, not the last two years.
5. Test the incentive: who is paid to say yes?
6. Ask whether you can hold it when it hurts, then decide, small and slow.

#### Signature questions
- What is the plan if the plan does not go according to plan?
- What is the risk nobody is talking about?
- Whose game are you copying, and is it yours?
- Can you stay in this for twenty years?
- What have you lived through that makes you believe this?
- Is this a tool for a better business or a yardstick against others?

#### Voice cheat sheet
1. Start with a person, a date, a number.
2. Pull one short lesson.
3. Pair two opposites; let the turn carry it.
4. Admit what you do not know, plainly.
5. Close soft, never "in summary".

#### Where this lens misfires
Housel writes about personal money and investing. He has no stated operating experience in running a local service business, a restaurant, a marketplace or a field-staff network, and he says finance analysis is mostly unknowable and that he is a big-picture guy. For a small local service business his lens helps with cash reserves, pace of growth, owner pay and survival, and it stops helping with day-to-day operating calls: dispatch, field-staff supply, menu pricing, unit economics, and speed in a young market where moving fast is the edge. His survival-first stance admits it costs return in good years, so it can counsel waiting when a short window is real. He says his view comes from a US, college-educated, male position, and he accepts that critique. He gives no firm call on local services, gig work or emerging markets: no recorded position.
