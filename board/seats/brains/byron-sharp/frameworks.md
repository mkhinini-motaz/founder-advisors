# Frameworks: Byron Sharp

Built from his blog posts (2005 to 2020), the Mi3 write-up of a 2022 talk and a 2025 podcast. Quick depth. Scoring lines are guidance inferred from his rules, not his own thresholds.

## 1. Double Jeopardy law
- What: bigger brands are bought by more people, and those people are slightly more loyal. The sales gap between big and small brands is mostly a customer-count gap.
- Parts: penetration (share of category buyers who buy at least once), purchase frequency, share of category requirements. All loyalty metrics rise with brand size.
- Use when: someone reads a loyalty, retention or share-of-wallet number, or claims a rival has "magic" loyalty.
- Score (guidance): if the loyalty gap between two brands is about what their sizes predict, there is nothing to explain. Deviations usually have structural causes (S044).
- Quote: "But the main driver of why you have so many more sales than the small brand is you have so many more customers." (S001)
- Sources: 7 (S001, S030, S035, S044, S045, S071, S084).

## 2. Mental and physical availability
- What: the theory behind his growth advice. Mental availability is the chance buyers notice or think of the brand in a buying situation. Physical availability is how easy the brand is to buy: store, pack, app, payment, delivery.
- Parts: memory structures and distinctive assets (mental); distribution, reach, ease of purchase (physical).
- Use when: choosing between "brand" and "performance" spend, or deciding what to fix first.
- Score (guidance): list the buying situations, then ask in how many the brand is present in the head and within reach.
- Quote: "It’s the chance that people think of you when they’re going into a buying situation, or that they notice you on the shelf, or they see you on screen." (S001)
- Sources: 6 (S001, S012, S035, S105, S148, S154).

## 3. Heavy buyer fallacy and the light-buyer rule
- What: heavy buyers are hard to nudge and not at risk of leaving, so targeting them shrinks the brand. The biggest uplift from advertising comes from a brand's lightest buyers, who are its largest group.
- Parts: Natural Monopoly law (bigger brands have more light category buyers in their base); two kinds of light buyer (unnamed, S098).
- Use when: a plan says focus on our best customers or golden households.
- Score (guidance): if the plan only reaches existing heavy buyers, it cannot add customers.
- Quote: "So the idea that heavy buyers of your brand (“golden households” or “super consumers”) are your best target is flawed. Dangerously simplistic." (S041)
- Sources: 4 (S041, S098, S125, S175).

## 4. Duplication of Purchase law
- What: a smaller brand shares a larger percentage of its customers with a bigger one than the reverse. Asymmetric switching is not weak loyalty.
- Use when: someone says customers who leave us go to X or worries about cannibalisation between own brands.
- Example (S044): Android 90 percent, iPhone 10 percent, each swap two customers: 20 percent of iPhone but 2 percent of Android.
- Quote: "it’s a common mistake to see asymmetric switching and assume that one brand has less loyalty." (S044)
- Sources: 2 (S044, S140).

## 5. Repertoire versus subscription markets
- What: in repertoire markets (FMCG, store choice, TV channels) a brand rarely has over 20 percent solely loyal buyers. In subscription markets (insurance, banking) it is rarely under 70 percent.
- Use when: deciding if a loyalty or retention tactic fits the category. Growth in repertoire markets is recruitment plus more purchases by very light buyers.
- Quote: "There is virtually no middle ground between these extremes." (S057)
- Sources: 2 (S030, S057).

## 6. Attitudes reflect behaviour
- What: people who buy more agree more with positive survey statements. Survey attachment is a result of buying, not its cause. Brand trackers improve after share gains.
- Use when: someone cites brand love, engagement, NPS or an equity score as a growth driver.
- Quote: "Or more succinctly: attitudes reflect loyalty." (S066)
- Sources: 6 (S027, S066, S117, S125, S136, S144).

## 7. NBD-Dirichlet as a stationary benchmark
- What: a model of steady purchase probabilities with random wobble. It predicts the ratios of light, medium and heavy buyers and the natural churn of "loyals" with no real change.
- Use when: an alarming metric (a headline that brands lost half their loyal customers). Test what would happen under stationary conditions first.
- Quote: "They are misinterpreting the natural wobble in people’s purchasing histories as real change in their loyalties." (S089)
- Sources: 6 (S030, S045, S089, S125, S127, S157).

## 8. Differentiation versus distinctiveness
- What: differentiation is a reason to buy; distinctiveness is looking like yourself and unlike rivals. Distinctiveness (branding) is defensible; differentiation is not, apart from patents.
- Use when: briefing a logo, pack, colour or campaign.
- Quote: "Being distinctive means “looking like you”. It doesn’t mean doing whacky things." (S153)
- Sources: 4 (S104, S140, S148, S153).

## 9. Advertising works through memory structures
- What: advertising mostly refreshes, sometimes builds, memory structures that raise the chance of the brand being noticed or recalled in buying situations. Judge it on that, not on intention shift.
- Quote: "The dominant way that advertising works is by refreshing, and occasionally building, memory structures that improve the chance of the brand being recalled and/or noticed in buying situations and hence bought." (S144)
- Sources: 4 (S090, S105, S121, S144).

## 10. Catch people when they fall
- What: activation (search, in-store display, promotions) catches buyers who are in market. Advertising decides whether the brand is in the consideration set, often years earlier.
- Quote: "Paid search, in store display, price promotions, special offers – all of these things are to catch people when they fall." (S012)
- Sources: 2 (S001, S012).

## 11. Reasons not to buy
- What: brands compete on mental and physical availability, with most options culled unnoticed. A feature that turns people away can matter more to sales than a reason to buy.
- Quote: "reasons not to buy, can therefore be much more important (to sales) than reasons to buy." (S154)
- Sources: 2 (S154, S070).

## 12. The Halo Effect and success studies
- What: studies that pick winners by outcome and then ask what traits they share produce attributions from past performance. A valid study needs hard measures, competitor comparison, blind researchers and a test against future results.
- Use when: a book or consultant says companies with purpose, culture or ideals grow faster.
- Quote: "many things we commonly claim drive business performance are simply attributions based on past performance" (S078)
- Sources: 3 (S078, S079, S082).

## 13. How science builds laws (his test for a "law")
- What: observe a pattern, replicate and extend it across countries and categories, then run experiments for cause and size, by independent teams. A law is quantified, states when it holds and fails, and rests on systematic evidence.
- Quote: "This requires lots of replications/extensions over different conditions" (S028)
- Sources: 5 (S021, S028, S068, S134, S168).

## 14. Five problems with mix modelling
- What: (1) too little variation in sales and ad spend, (2) correlation taken as cause, (3) subjective choice of variables, (4) subjective choices about dynamics, (5) choosing by best fit to history, which does not predict.
- Quote: "In stark contrast, scientists look for simple models that hold under a wide range of conditions." (S168)
- Sources: 3 (S028, S168, S039).

## 15. Penetration and share of category requirements
- What: penetration is the size of the customer base; share of category requirements is how much of a buyer's category buying goes to the brand. A seven-point gain in share of wallet for a big retailer would equal doubling market share.
- Quote: "Penetration = the proportion of customers who buy your brand at least once in a given period. It’s the size of the brand’s customer base." (S084)
- Sources: 2 (S084, S030).

## 16. Stores compete for shopping trips
- What: a store grows by winning a larger share of shopping trips, not by selling more items to the same trips. A wide range is the tail that wins every shopper type; cut only items shoppers substitute without noticing.
- Quote: "stores compete in terms of selling items" (S070)
- Sources: 2 (S070, S071).

## Books

### Books: Byron Sharp

Built from his blog (2005 to 2020), a 2022 talk write-up and a 2025 podcast. Book text is never reproduced here; this is a reading from the sources.

#### How Brands Grow (his)
- Core thesis: brands grow by adding customers, not by squeezing loyal ones. Scientific laws (Double Jeopardy, Natural Monopoly, Duplication of Purchase) describe how markets behave, and mental and physical availability fit them.
- Frameworks in his words: loyalty-program research (chapter 12, per S001), mental availability and its measurement (part 2, per S030, S105, S148), the Natural Monopoly law (page 97, per S098).
- Taught in: S001, S030, S035, S043, S098, S105, S148.
- Note: S079 gives it as his pick for marketing, "but I’m not a disinterested source." (S079)

#### Marketing: theory, evidence, practice (textbook)
- Announced in 2012 as "A University marketing textbook that reflects modern evidence." (S069)

#### Books he recommends (S043, S078, S079)
- Thinking Fast and Slow (Kahneman), The Halo Effect (Rosenzweig), Decoded (Barden), Applying Scientific Thinking to Marketing (Grapentine), Everything is Obvious (Watts), Viral Marketing: the science of sharing (Nelson-Field), The Advertising Effect.
- On general corporate success he points to The Halo Effect: "Yes. The Halo Effect if you are interested in the general area of corporate success." (S079)

#### Books he criticises
- Grow (Stengel): flawed study, circular logic (S078, S079).
- The 22 Immutable Laws of Marketing (Ries and Trout): "quaint platitudes" and anecdotes dressed as laws (S134).
- How Customers Think (Zaltman): an advertisement for a patented technique (S143).
- Purple Cow (Godin): repackaged orthodoxy (S092).
- Marketing textbooks in general: "Marketing textbooks seldom contain substantive research findings, let alone new research." (S178)
