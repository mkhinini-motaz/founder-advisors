# Frameworks: Tobi Lütke

Built from 11 sources (podcasts, talks, interviews, a letter; 2011 to 2026) at quick depth. Source counts are counts of the sources in the notes that use the framework. Scoring lines are guidance, not his words.

## 1. Remove friction, merchant success is Shopify's growth (the red button)
- What: find the step where a would-be entrepreneur gives up, remove it, and success rates rise across the platform. Shopify's job is to approximate a "red button" that gives anyone a profitable side business. Revenue is a side effect.
- Use when: any product, fee or policy decision that touches a small merchant.
- Score (guidance): does the change make a hard step simpler for a merchant, and does it raise how many merchants succeed? If it adds a step to extract money, no.
- Quote: "every single time we do, we see success rates across the platform go up and expand the market size." (S018)
- Also: "every single time we made something significantly simpler, it actually caused more success." (S003)
- Sources: S001, S003, S017, S018, S031, S035 (6).

## 2. Same side of the table
- What: build incentives so the customer's success is the company's growth. Share in merchants' success, support partners, do not carve out their profits.
- Use when: choosing a fee model, a partner program or an app marketplace.
- Score (guidance): if the platform earns when the merchant does not, the incentive is on the wrong side.
- Quote: "we are actually on the same side of the table with our customers." (S003)
- Sources: S003, S017, S018, S004 (4).

## 3. Pricing so the best merchants stay
- What: his first model took a percentage of sales, which taxed the merchants he most wanted. He moved to a monthly subscription plus a low transaction fee. He also says a charge focuses the customer.
- Use when: setting commission or subscription for the heaviest users.
- Score (guidance): does the fee rise sharply with the success of the best customers? Then they will leave or resent you.
- Quote: "we essentially made it so the people who would use it a lot would pay way too much, and the people who wouldn’t really need it would get it for free." (S004)
- Sources: S004, S001, S035 (3).

## 4. Customers explain problems, builders solve them
- What: listen to merchants for the problem, not for the design. Merchants asked for inventory sync; the real answer was a point of sale on the same database.
- Use when: a merchant asks for a feature.
- Score (guidance): can you restate the request as a goal? If not, you have not understood it.
- Quote: "You're solving the right problem but in the wrong way." (S018)
- Also: "Customers are not the people who should say what needs to be built." (S003)
- Sources: S003, S018, S001 (3).

## 5. Ask what people are actually trying to accomplish
- What: do not mistake today's solution for the goal. Online stores were a soft bed under the real goal, independence. His model case is the shipping container: the aim was moving goods, not trucks.
- Quote: "They care about selling. They care about becoming successful entrepreneurs. They care about reaching for independence and the success of that" (S001)
- Sources: S001, S018, S002 (3).

## 6. Grow the market, do not fight for share
- What: in 2008 a VC passed on a market of 40,000 online stores. Shopify existed because the friction kept that number low. He later said the VCs may have been right on the market as it stood.
- Use when: sizing a market that you intend to change.
- Quote: "Shopify grew its market instead of competing for percentage in a market." (S002)
- Sources: S002, S018, S031 (3).

## 7. Environment over policy; floor versus ceiling
- What: incentives, policies and culture shape a company, but the environment is stronger; for software firms the internal tools are the environment. Policies raise the floor and lower the ceiling.
- Use when: someone proposes a new rule, approval step or recurring meeting.
- Score (guidance): can you change the environment so the right thing is intuitive? If yes, skip the policy. Accept policy only where a mistake would be severe.
- Quote: "They bring up the floor, so no one actually does something really, really wrong. But what people don’t see is, they also bring down the ceiling." (S003)
- Also: "it’s the environment that’s even more powerful than all those things" (S031)
- Sources: S003, S031, S033 (3).

## 8. Subtraction and sediment layers
- What: adding is cheaper than removing; each addition was a good idea, together they make bureaucracy. Delete recurring meetings on a schedule. A yes to one thing is a no to everything else.
- Quote: "The best thing founders can do is subtraction." (S035)
- Also: "We periodically delete all recurring meetings because it seems very hard for people to subtract." (S003)
- Sources: S003, S035 (2).

## 9. Company as a programmable system (GSD, desired state)
- What: put the real state of the company in a legible register (every project, deadline, change) and review it often; later, hold a model of the company as code against reality. Move reality toward the model.
- Use when: you cannot see what your team is working on.
- Quote: "Let’s get the actual state of a business into a legible internal system, by which you can reason about it." (S031)
- Also: "I need a desired state system for this company." (S033)
- Sources: S002, S004, S031, S033, S035 (5).

## 10. Boxes
- What: life and companies are a series of boxes. Enter, probe, get comfortable, then look for the crack to a larger box. Comfort is the danger. Project work has boxes with phase transitions where he takes risk in return for team autonomy.
- Quote: "And then comes the dangerous part because it’s now comfortable again." (S001)
- Also: "It’s like a trade-off of accountability for autonomy." (S033)
- Sources: S001, S033, S037 (3).

## 11. Hardest customer is your gym
- What: do not fire the customer whose load breaks you; rebuild the system for the peak. A t-shirt drop crashed Shopify around 2010; later Supreme and Kylie Jenner lip kits did the same.
- Quote: "We are using this as a gym and we’re going to use them." (S031)
- Sources: S031, S018 (2).

## 12. Fall in love with problems, not solutions
- What: the biggest difference between people, hard to see in calm times. He induces change to find out.
- Quote: "some people fall in love with solutions. Some people fall in love with problems." (S031)
- Sources: S001, S031, S033 (3).

## 13. Small teams: one, then five
- What: one author is the best team, five the second best; each step up costs a lot of output. Work bigger than one book needs a team with one editor.
- Quote: "The second best size of a team is five" (S002)
- Sources: S002, S033 (2).

## 14. Rivalry versus competition
- What: a rivalry galvanises both sides; a direct fight loses time to a third party. Study rivals for what they teach, not out of envy. He admits the distinction may matter less than he claims.
- Quote: "Galvanizing a rivalry is good for both sides." (S002)
- Sources: S002, S033 (2).

## 15. Re-derive when the premise breaks
- What: when a core assumption fails, throw out the plan and rebuild the decision tree. In COVID he categorised all work as helpful or not, and cancelled about 60% of projects. Keep the most important thing the most important thing.
- Quote: "you should always prune back the decision tree all the way there and go forward." (S033)
- Also: "the most important thing is to keep the most important thing, the most important thing." (S035)
- Sources: S033, S035 (2).

## 16. First 80% / last 20%
- What: the first 80% of every commerce business is the same; build composable primitives and let merchants do the unique last 20%.
- Quote: "The first 80% of every e-commerce business are the same." (S031)
- Sources: S031 (1).

## 17. Build what you want to exist
- What: build the tool you need, then assume more people like you exist. He calls building only what others like "the silent killer".
- Quote: "We were our own customers. We extrapolated that there were more people like us." (S004)
- Sources: S002, S003, S004, S033 (4).

## Books

### Books: Tobi Lütke

Built from 11 sources, 2011 to 2026. Lütke has written no book. His written work is the 2015 IPO letter and the April 2025 AI memo to staff (both in S017).

Books he names, in my words:
- High Output Management (Andy Grove): management as an engineering exercise, which made being CEO less scary. (S001)
- The Box (Marc Levinson): logistics history, to understand the shipping container. (S001)
- Mindset (Carol Dweck): fixed versus growth mindset. (S001)
- Influence (Cialdini): people can be influenced, so framing and story matter. (S001)
- Seeing Like a State (James C. Scott): how legibility goes wrong. (S003)
- A Conflict of Visions (Thomas Sowell): humans are limited, so tradeoffs. (S003)
- The Lean Startup (Eric Ries): good ideas, but in retail it tells builders to follow customers over vision. (S003)
- Team of Teams (McChrystal): the model for his project review system. (S002)
- Punished by Rewards (Alfie Kohn) and Drive (Dan Pink): motivation is intrinsic. (S018)
- 1,000 True Fans (Kevin Kelly): the internet lets niche products find their fans. (S003, S031)
- Parkinson's Law: he gives it to executives. (S035)
- Against the Odds (James Dyson): differentiate and keep control. (S033)
- Management books in general: "the business books are largely written by the people who have time, who are not the people who actually build companies" (S033)
