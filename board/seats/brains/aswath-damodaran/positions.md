# Positions: Aswath Damodaran

Built from 65 of his blog posts (2011 to 2026). The corpus is investor-side writing, so many founder topics have no recorded position. Where he changed his mind, both views appear with dates and the newer one is marked.

## Pricing a product or service
No recorded position on how a founder should price a product. On how markets price companies, see "Value versus price" below.

## Value versus price
- "VC success is measured based on price at entry and price at exit on an investment, rather than the quality of the business built" (S004, 2026). Earlier, same view: "Venture capitalists (VCs) don't value companies, they price them!" (S042, 2016). Stable across the whole corpus.

## Raising money and venture capital
- VCs are built to price, not to build businesses: "it is unrealistic to expect them to do the heavy lifting of building businesses for the long term" (S004, 2026). He says it is a reading of incentives, not an attack on the people.
- Headline valuations of private companies deserve a grain of salt: "public investors should take the numbers that are often bandied about as valuations of private companies (like Palantir, Uber and Airbnb) with a grain of salt" (S042, 2016). Structure (ratchets, options) hides overpayment (S053, S042).
- VC target returns: "the cynic in you should wonder whether these numbers are marketing props, rather than financial metrics." (S001, 2026)

## Debt and leverage
- Extreme leverage turns equity into an option: "you are investing less in an ongoing business, and more in an out-of-the-money option" (S031, 2019). Treat leases as debt (S031). Young high-growth firms should not borrow or buy back stock (S011). Flexibility in financing helped in the 2020 crisis (S028).

## Hiring, key people and firing a CEO
- "the most valuable key people find ways to make themselves more dispensable and less valuable over time" (S013, 2023).
- No one template fits: "it is possible that the quality that binds together successful CEOs the most is luck" (S020, 2021). Fit changes by life cycle stage (S011, S020, S040).
- CEO pay: "an AI-generated CEO could replace the CEOs of half or more of the S&P 500 companies, and no one would notice the difference." (S013, 2023)
- First hire and team building: no recorded position.

## Partners
No recorded position.

## Equity, control and dual-class shares
- Opposed, consistently from 2019 to 2026. "continues the deplorable practice of entrenching founders" (S031, 2019). Earlier, more resigned: founder voting rights bother him but are now par for the course in young tech (S033, 2019). Newer and harder: "a voting share design that is so deviously self-serving that it would put Kim Jong Un to shame." (S002, 2026)

## Focus versus diversification
- Company side: a focused story can beat a big one, but a big story gets a higher price if believed. "if you don’t make it, you will disappoint them, no matter how good your numbers may be." (S051, 2015). Later he preferred Lyft's focus to Uber's overreach (S033, 2019).
- Investor side: diversify to survive uncertainty; the law of large numbers is robust (S046, S001).

## Niche versus big market
- "Big market stories generally yield higher valuation and pricing than small market stories!" (S032, 2019), and the danger is to over-value: "the danger to investors, when faced with Indian and Chinese companies, is not that they will under value these companies, but that they will over value them, precisely because the markets are so big" (S023, 2021). In 2014 he used too small a market for Uber and was wrong; he now errs toward the expanded market for disruptors (S030, S057).

## Brand
- A brand shows up in operating margin: "a company that claims that it has a valuable brand name, while delivering operating margins well below the industry average, really does not" (S014, 2023).

## Content, paid ads and remote work, work hours, burnout
No recorded position on any of these.

## AI
- Newest view (2026): bubbles are expected in revolutionary change. "Thus, bubbles are a feature, not a bug, when revolutionary change is a possibility." (S005, 2026)
- AI as a tool lowers costs for everyone and competition then pushes prices and margins down; larger firms gain (S007, 2026). Subscription pricing fails when the marginal cost of a user is not zero (S005).
- "AI has built the most expensive factory in history, and done so in hyper speed." (S005, 2026) He calls himself an AI novice.

## Selling the company and acquisitions
- Four reasons for a deal: pricing game, real option, synergy, defense. "Defensive mergers, though, are a sign of weakness, not strength, and point to a business model under stress." (S034, 2018) Synergy claims usually come "generally with nothing to back it up" (S034).

## Culture and ethics
- Skeptic of corporate virtue: "much of that discussion is akin to painting lipstick on a pig" (S007, 2026). Earlier on ESG: "I remain skeptical about ESG, but will hold off on passing judgment on whether this is just a public relations ploy" (S024, 2020). Newer view is harder: "the effect of ESG on value, no matter what its advocates claim, is non-existent." (S014, 2023)
- Profit as the end game: "Crass and mercantile though this may sound, the end game for a business is to make money" (S007, 2026).

## Metrics
- Take rate, unit economics and margin matter more than users or GMV: "I would talk less about users and gross merchandise value, and focus more on improving the abysmally low take rate at the firm" (S020, 2021). Adjusted profit measures are suspect (S031, S019, S038). Annualized run rates deserve suspicion (S003).

## Competition and moats
- In tech, moats mean stickiness: "stickiness implies that customers will not switch to alternatives, even if these alternatives are technically superior and cheaper." (S001, 2026). Moats have crumbled over four decades (S007).

## Marketplaces and platforms
- A platform's value rests on market-level drivers and on whether it has intense users and proprietary data (S023, S036). Growth-only models rarely show economies of scale (S038, S036).

## Timing
- "I am not a market timer" (S015, 2023). Do not use the implied premium for timing. In IPOs, mood and momentum decide the first day, not value (S014, S024).

## Disruption
- "disruption is easy, but making money on disruption is hard." (S050, 2015)

## Regulation and the gig-contractor model
- Expects drivers to become partial or full employees, lowering margins (S033, S052, S030).

## Country and emerging-market risk
- "there is no place to hide from country risk, for either businesses or investors" (S006, 2026). Earlier the same: country risk is not diversifiable as correlations rise (S062, 2013).

## Growth and negative growth
- "Our unwillingness to consider using negative growth in valuation has turned the game over to growth advocates." (S040, 2016)

## Margin of safety
- "an excuse for inaction rather than a guide to action in risky times." (S047, 2016), and "MOS is not an alternative to any existing risk measure used in valuation but an add-on" (S046, 2016). Newer, in 2026, he still sizes it by how many opportunities you see and how much cash you hold (S001).

## Trading and momentum
- "Put bluntly, if you live by momentum, you die by it." (S019, 2022). "trading is not my game, and I have neither the tools nor the inclination to play it" (S022, 2021).

## Cash burn
- "If you are an investor in a company that is burning through cash, don't panic!" (S043, 2016)

## Disclosure and prospectuses
- "it is long and filled with distractions, as has become the case with almost every initial public offering in the last two decades." (S002, 2026)

## Markets and efficiency
- "Markets are not all knowing and they are definitely not efficient, but they are extraordinary platforms for conveying a consensus view of the future" (S025, 2020).
