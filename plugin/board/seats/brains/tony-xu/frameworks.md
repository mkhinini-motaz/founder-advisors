# Frameworks: Tony Xu

Built from 45 of his public sources (talks, interviews, lectures, earnings calls, a shareholder letter, 2016 to 2026). Source counts are counts of the sources in the notes that use the framework. Scoring lines are guidance, not his words.

## 1. Operating at the lowest level of detail (five whys, do the work yourself)
- What: do the job yourself until you find the real problem below the surface. A delivery breaks into about 20 steps, each with its own delays; you cannot list the causes from outside, only by doing the work. Ask five whys down a hypothesis tree to the root cause, fix each root operationally, and only then write software.
- Steps: do the work (deliveries, support); write down what you think the problem is; go lower on the tree; fix the roots; automate last.
- Use when: any recurring complaint, late order, or ops metric that looks fine but feels wrong.
- Score (guidance): can the person deciding name the step in the chain that fails, from having done it? If not, no go on a fix.
- Quote: "operating at the lowest level of detail is pretty much trying to find your way to the right problem and it almost never is at the surface." (S001)
- Also: "there's about 20 steps you can decompose a delivery into" (S066); "you start by doing the work yourself." (S074)
- Sources: S001, S006, S007, S036, S037, S039, S040, S052, S053, S066, S071, S074, S077, S078, S079, S080, S085 (17).

## 2. Three questions before scaling
- What: strip an idea to the simplest test. Would customers pay (about six dollars)? Would merchants pay (about 15%)? Would drivers see a job worth taking at a wage you can afford? Stay in two markets until all three are proven. In 2024 he named the consumer question as the biggest fear.
- Use when: opening a new city, category or service.
- Score (guidance): three yes answers backed by repeat behaviour, not by discounts. Any "not yet" means stay small.
- Quote: "until we prove those three points we actually didn't" (S047)
- Also: "we always had three questions but the biggest question was always consumers probably just didn't want this otherwise it would have existed" (S079)
- Sources: S001, S002, S006, S037, S047, S066, S079, S080, S083, S085, S090 (11).

## 3. Works for every audience (three-sided orchestration)
- What: a marketplace works only if it works for consumer, merchant and courier at once, and product-market fit is found separately for each. If one side has a reason to avoid another, the product is wrong.
- Use when: a pay change, fee change, new category or any promotion.
- Score (guidance): list the three sides and the effect on each. A move that costs one side to help another fails.
- Quote: "DoorDash only works if it works for every single participant" (S045)
- Also: "you kind of have to find product market fit with three audiences" (S083)
- Sources: S011, S039, S042, S045, S046, S053, S062, S066, S078, S083 (10).

## 4. Inputs, not outcomes
- What: pick the inputs you control (selection, speed, accuracy, affordability, behaviours) and let the score follow. Even on IPO day the weekly business review followed the bell. Judge any use of time or money by what it returns.
- Quote: "it's always focusing on the inputs not the outcomes." (S001)
- Also: "output to input ratio" (S080)
- Sources: S001, S037, S040, S055, S080 (5).

## 5. Do things that don't scale, then experiment, then productize
- What: hand work shows the pattern. A problem seen more than once becomes an experiment, then a product. Never automate before the pattern exists.
- Quote: "you'd be automating something that doesn’t even exist." (S001)
- Sources: S001, S006, S037, S066 (4).

## 6. Experiment volume
- What: tens of thousands of experiments a year, most fail, the few that work compound for everyone. Data settles debates, but the best ideas come from anecdotes at the edges of the distribution. Early on, the same shape: about 100 ideas tried, three or four did better than the other 90.
- Quote: "tries to have experiments and data settle the debate" (S040)
- Also: "that's why most experiments fail" (S066)
- Sources: S006, S038, S040, S066, S080 (5).

## 7. Systems, not products (state machine, mini systems)
- What: a system is an orchestration of products with shared state. Each component has its own objective, and managing to component goals alone fails. Manage the handoffs. A heroic fix is an exception, not a system. Watch the delivery as a state machine (green to red) with prevention models plus a fast response, like air traffic control. New tech (AVs, AI agents) joins the system and does not replace it.
- Quote: "carefully manage the complex coordination of the handoffs between components" (S007)
- Also: "is not a system. That is an exception." (S090); "there's almost like 20 mini systems, many little processes, if you will" (S071)
- Sources: S007, S071, S080, S085, S090 (5).

## 8. Hardest problem first, crawl walk run
- What: start with the highest-frequency, most perishable category in the hardest conditions. Density is greatest, and the easier categories follow. Seven years on food before groceries.
- Quote: "we like to solve the hardest problems first" (S039)
- Also: "it was one of one in terms of the network that you needed to build to get right" (S079)
- Sources: S001, S037, S039, S071, S079 (5).

## 9. A city is the unit of business
- What: make one city work, then the second, then baskets of cities. Each city is learned from scratch; once you learn what makes a type of city work, it becomes a formula and a checklist of about 50 activities. Start small before you scale.
- Quote: "for us, that was a city" (S052)
- Also: "you first have to start small before you can scale very quickly and very big" (S076)
- Sources: S002, S019, S047, S052, S076, S078, S080 (7).

## 10. Conviction plus evidence
- What: a contrarian bet (suburbs) is tested by cities two, three, four and five repeating the same behaviour. Retention and frequency are the honest check because they cannot be faked. In 2024 he put his own conviction at about year four, when football-town cities worked as well as big ones.
- Quote: "you also need evidence along the way to make sure that you’re not delusional." (S002)
- Sources: S002, S006, S040, S079 (4).

## 11. Two operating systems (big airplane, paper airplanes)
- What: the core business improves by small compounding gains. New bets get their own goals, measures, talent, incentives and timelines. Every project is a startup, and you earn the right to the next stage as customers show it works. Respect core work as much as the shiny new thing.
- Quote: "you need very different goals, goaling systems and incentive systems." (S006)
- Also: "you kind of have to earn your right to the next stage" (S066); "you have to build the core and you have to build the new." (S074)
- Sources: S006, S038, S040, S066, S071, S074 (6).

## 12. Disconfirming evidence (truth seeking)
- What: meetings start from written docs that guess at the problem and look for what disproves it. Bring data and insight, not opinion. Everyone, support staff included, learns SQL to pull their own data.
- Quote: "we’re really looking for the disconfirming evidence" (S001)
- Also: "we taught everyone SQL for example including our customer support" (S079)
- Sources: S001, S037, S039, S042, S079 (5).

## 13. Win-win-win test for a new category
- What: add a category only if it helps consumers, merchants and Dashers together, and test the delivery system for years before a regulated one such as alcohol.
- Quote: "alcohol you know really presents a win-win-win for all of our audiences" (S046)
- Also: "alcohol truly is a win-win-win" (S075)
- Sources: S046, S058, S075 (3).

## 14. Look one click lower than the average
- What: an average hides the task. Ask whether pay is right for this task, then whether supply is oversaturated. A courier avoiding a merchant is a product failure.
- Quote: "we have to look one click lower in my opinion which is is it right for the right task" (S053)
- Sources: S053, S045, S062 (3).

## 15. Fire yourself every six months
- What: at each inflection, understand the problem yourself, find someone better to solve it, move on, and take the next hole. A great leader matched to the wrong problem fails. Moving people every six months worked to about 1,500 employees, then got harder.
- Quote: "whenever you match um a leader that is phenomenal in their own right to the wrong problem you don't always get the best outcome" (S039)
- Also: "you're gonna have to learn to fire yourself every six months" (S072)
- Sources: S039, S040, S072, S079 (4).

## 16. Culture is 80% who you are
- What: write down the behaviours and stories of the first 10 to 20 people, then turn them into principles (six operating principles in 2024). Repeat them in every channel. Customer obsession is shown by actions, not said.
- Quote: "culture is 80 who you are and 20 who you aspire to be" (S055)
- Sources: S040, S055, S079, S090 (4).

## 17. Crisis order: safety, liquidity, community
- What: in a crisis, safety first, then cash, then the community; in the three bad years he kept one list of three goals, all required.
- Also: "We got to keep growing and keep taking share. We got to get more profitable and we can't run out of cash." (S066)
- Sources: S001, S011, S066, S075, S084 (5).

## 18. Order density (frequency times store coverage)
- What: the asset is last-mile logistics for every local business. The lowest cost needs order density; density needs many stores and frequent use; restaurants are the biggest and most frequent category, so they came first. Hub and spoke: suburban main streets with homes on the spokes beat dense city centres on delivery time.
- Use when: choosing a first category, a zone, or whether a new line can pay for riders.
- Score (guidance): can you name the stores and the weekly frequency that fill a courier's hour in the zone? If not, wait.
- Quote: "most important metric is order density and that's actually why dorash started in restaurants" (S082)
- Also: "you need really order density and in order to get order density you need coverage of stores and you need high frequency of activity" (S079); "you needed network density." (S066)
- Sources: S062, S066, S079, S082 (4).

## 19. Sequencing game (improve your position)
- What: know the endgame, work backwards, and pick each step by whether it improves your position. Restaurant delivery took seven or eight steps and nine years. Compete against an incumbent where it is not set up to follow, not on its ground.
- Quote: "a sequencing game where the name of the game is to improve your position" (S079)
- Also: "you can't compete against an incumbent on their territory you're going to lose" (S079); "the sequencing of the activities are very important" (S076)
- Sources: S076, S079 (2).

## 20. Start from the customer's end-to-end job (customer-only metrics)
- What: define the whole job first, then pick the interface or the technology. Run the company on measures only a customer would care about. A digital layer that cannot do the physical part does not own the job.
- Use when: judging a new tool, an AI feature, a data-sharing deal.
- Score (guidance): does it finish the whole sentence the customer said? If not, it is a part, not a product.
- Quote: "And our job's not finished until that whole sentence gets completed." (S074)
- Also: "are we solving the end-to-end job? If we are not solving the end-to-end job, it's not going to matter." (S090)
- Sources: S006, S007, S074, S090 (4).

## 21. War for atoms versus war for bits
- What: two kinds of battle, attention (bits) and the physical world (atoms). Play the atoms and partner with the attention companies; the physical world is where everything is an edge case, and where customer outcomes decide how many shots new technology gets.
- Quote: "We are in the war for atoms because, you know, one day ultimately like what's the point of having a personal assistant if it can't actually do real things." (S071)
- Sources: S071, S074 (2).

## 22. Ship the smallest test first
- What: a static page with eight PDF menus, a Google Voice number ringing the founders' phones, a card reader, Find My Friends as dispatch. The test was the question (would anyone order), not the polish of the interface. Convince yourselves first.
- Use when: any new idea, city, category or fee.
- Score (guidance): can you run it in hours with the founders as the system? If it needs a build, shrink it.
- Quote: "whenever you can ship something in 43 minutes to test your idea" (S066)
- Also: "But the test wasn't how low-friction the UI was." (S080); "until you actually do something you know to actually collect real information all the analysis isn't really worth that much" (S085)
- Sources: S066, S080, S083, S085 (4).

## 23. Marketplace plus tools (merchant owns its channel)
- What: two assets for a merchant: a marketplace that brings new customers, and the same tools so the merchant runs its own delivery and digital channel. The marketplace is not meant to be the merchant's only way.
- Quote: "doordash has really built you know two assets to partner with retailers" (S075)
- Also: "so that they don't have to rely on us" (S037); "by building this app that you know can bring you incremental customers but also by giving you tools right" (S084)
- Sources: S037, S075, S076, S082, S084 (5).

## 24. Hiring tests (eight-hour test, back-door checks, names)
- What: the final interview is twenty minutes of questions and then a task: eight hours and $20 to get 100 customers. Do 20 to 25 blind back-door checks per senior hire, skip levels most. For every offer, write out the names of the people the candidate beats. Listen for evidence; there is no silver-bullet question. Bet on talent and motivation over experience.
- Score (guidance): has the candidate shown action and detail unprompted, and do skip-level references agree?
- Quote: "every hire that I've made I probably do at least 20 to 25 back door checks" (S079)
- Also: "I'm going to give you $20 that you can use to go and acquire 100 customers for us" (S066)
- Sources: S038, S066, S079, S085 (4).

## 25. Restaurant dollar and incrementality
- What: a restaurant's costs are people, food and real estate, roughly a third each with about ten cents left. A delivery order reuses the kitchen and the rent, so the incremental margin is higher, if the sale is new business. Judge any program by how much of it is incremental.
- Score (guidance): ask the partner what share of the sale would not otherwise exist; his own figures were estimates.
- Quote: "there's three major costs. There's people, there's food, and there's real estate." (S077)
- Also: "70 to 90 percent of these sales are incremental they're sales that they otherwise would not have received" (S082); "you're going to make 3-5x the incremental margin" (S090)
- Sources: S077, S082, S090 (3).

### 26. Distribution, not the average
- What: judge service by the spread of customer experiences, not the mean. A late tail ruins the customer even when the average looks good.
- Use when: reading any delivery-time, wait or accuracy dashboard.
- Score (guidance): ask for the worst decile and the tail causes; a good average with a bad tail is a fail.
- Quote: "Averages in our industry are meaningless, it’s the distribution that matters." (S099)
- Sources: S099 (1); related to entry 14 (S053).

### 27. "And" solutions against false either/or
- What: search for answers that win on both sides of a supposed trade-off. Example: cut waste in logistics (Dashers never wait, items never sit idle) and both growth and profitability improve. They come from many small details with a good risk and reward, not a silver bullet.
- Use when: a team presents a choice between speed and cost, growth and profit, merchants and couriers.
- Score (guidance): has the team named the waste that serves both sides? If the choice stays binary after the check, say what was not found.
- Quote: "solving for growth “or” profitability can be a false dichotomy" (S100)
- Also: "looking for “and” solutions that fight false “either/or” dichotomies" (S099)
- Sources: S099, S100 (2).

### 28. Edge cases are the routine (predict, prevent, respond)
- What: at about seven million deliveries a day, a one-in-a-million event happens seven times a day; the out-of-stock jar, the missed gate code and the congested lot are daily. Build observability, a single source of truth, alerting and response tools; most days it looks overbuilt and on bad days it is what saves you. Example: SafeChat+, which uses AI to judge when a physical incident might occur.
- Use when: designing support, safety or dispatch tools; judging whether an ops system is "too much".
- Score (guidance): can the system predict and prevent the frequent small failures and respond instantly to the rare large one?
- Quote: "In traditional software engineering, these examples might be viewed as edge cases. In our world, they are the routine." (S100)
- Also: "we’ve built observability, a single source of truth, alerting, and response tools" (S100)
- Sources: S100 (1); related to entry 7 and principle 10 (S053, S071).

### 29. Four-step operating approach and four pillars (2025 letter)
- What: four steps: focus on the customer; make small investments to prove product-market fit and only then double down; operate at the lowest level of detail (edge cases, and-solutions); take the long view. Four pillars behind the year: improve inputs to the product, keep a high bar before large capital, improve unit economics as the product improves, keep getting more efficient.
- Use when: deciding whether to fund a new line, and how much.
- Score (guidance): has the line shown product-market fit with small money? If not, no big capital.
- Quote: "2/ make small investments to prove product market fit, and only then double down" (S100)
- Also: "only investing significant capital after we see signs of product market fit" (S100)
- Sources: S099, S100 (2).

### 30. What makes a great business (product leap, retention, unit economics, enduring trend)
- What: a product a substantial leap better than today's, strong retention and engagement, a path to strong unit economics, and a trend that makes a large market and will last. Cohort check: each new cohort expects more and engages less, so a new cohort that engages more is the evidence.
- Use when: judging a new category or a new city.
- Score (guidance): four yes answers; the product leap and retention are the weight.
- Quote: "great businesses tend to start from great products (usually a substantial leap better than what customers are accustomed to today)" (S100)
- Sources: S100 (1).

### 31. Attributes of Excellence (hiring and culture)
- What: high accountability and ownership; bias for action; lowest level of detail; hold two opposing truths without being dogmatic; track record of building teams; followership (count how many people follow a candidate when they change jobs). Hire by listening, not by a magic question.
- Use when: judging an operator or a senior hire.
- Score (guidance): has the candidate shown action and detail unprompted, and do people follow them?
- Quote: "we call them the attributes of Excellence that we look for in building a culture" (S091)
- Also: "I don't think there are any magical interview questions I think most of these things you know know happen through listening" (S091)
- Sources: S091 (1); related to entry 24.

### 32. Merchant services stack
- What: logistics, merchant services (customer acquisition, delivery, insights, merchandising, payments, support) and a membership (DashPass, flat monthly fee) as three assets that reinforce each other. Logistics alone does not help a merchant compete.
- Quote: "Logistics alone, however, will not help physical businesses compete." (S099)
- Sources: S099 (1); related to entry 23.

## Books

### Books: Tony Xu

Built from 45 public sources, 2016 to 2026. Xu has written no book.

He has no books of his own. His major written work is the series of DoorDash shareholder letters; the Q4 2025 letter (S007, February 2026) sets out his systems-versus-products view in full. He rarely names books: the 2016 to 2024 talks (S072, S078 to S085, S090) credit none to him.

Books named around him (S006, 2026; S066, 2026):
- Zero to One (Peter Thiel): he says successful people find value in unexpected places and think from first principles, not formulas. (S006) In S066 the host quotes it and Xu is not credited.
- The Wright Brothers (David McCullough): rivals had more money, the brothers won with bicycle-shop profits. He uses it to argue for finding a problem worth solving first (S006, S066).
- Sam Walton's autobiography: raised by the host, not by Xu, as an "earned secret" (S006).

Top-up (batch 07): S091 (2024) adds only that he reads non-fiction, mostly history and biography of real people, including military leaders and company histories that tell the story forward as it happened; he names no title. The S-1 letter (S099, 2020) and the Q4 2024 letter (S100, January 2025) are further written work: the S-1 sets out five values and three assets; the 2024 letter sets out four pillars and four steps.
