# Frameworks: Annie Duke

Built from 44 usable sources (about 258,000 words): her Substack, podcasts, interviews. Most-used first. Scoring lines are guidance drawn from her thresholds, not her words.

## 1. Resulting
Judging a decision by its outcome. Outcome quality and decision quality are only loosely linked, because luck and hidden information sit between them. She tests it with a thought experiment: flip the outcome and see whether the headline flips.
- Use: any post-mortem, hiring or firing review, strategy review after a win or loss.
- Score: ask: if this had gone the other way, would I still call it a bad decision? If the verdict flips, it is resulting.
- Quote: "Sometimes we equate the quality of a decision with the quality of an outcome, which is a fallacy called resulting." (S067)
- Sources: S067, S071, S074, S076, S077, S080, S087, S088, S090, S096, S101, S093 (12).

## 2. Decisions as bets / the three P's
Every decision is a forecast. Map the possible futures, attach a payoff and a probability to each, then read the expected value and the downside you could not survive.
- Use: any choice with uncertain results; replaces the pros-and-cons list.
- Score: guidance from S088: for each upside and downside ask how big and how probable; once expectancy is positive, move to bet sizing.
- Quote: "A decision is a bet on a particular future." (S067)
- Sources: S067, S071, S087, S088, S091, S101 (6).

## 3. Kill criteria (states and dates)
Before you start, imagine it going badly and write down the signals that mean you must stop. The best ones name a state and a date. Pair with a premortem and a quitting coach.
- Use: projects, hires, investments, sites or lines of business that may need closing.
- Score: guidance: a criterion without a state, a date and an action is not yet a kill criterion.
- Quote: "the best kill criteria contain a state and a date" (S081)
- Sources: S080, S081, S082, S089, S091, S093, S057 (7).

## 4. Sunk cost as a forward-looking problem
What is spent is gone; only the next dollar or hour matters. The test: knowing what you know today, would you start this today?
- Use: any ongoing project that keeps asking for more.
- Score: if the honest answer to the start-it-today question is no, stop.
- Quote: "waste is a forward-looking problem, not a backward-looking problem" (S079)
- Sources: S025, S079, S080, S088, S091 (5).

## 5. Monkeys and pedestals (from Astro Teller)
Attack the hard, unknown bottleneck (the monkey) before the easy, known work (the pedestal). Easy wins first are false progress and make quitting harder.
- Use: roadmaps, launches, new product lines.
- Score: list the one thing that, if it fails, makes the rest pointless; has it been tested?
- Quote: "Monkeys and pedestals tells us to attack the hard thing first because if we can’t solve for that there is really no point in doing the rest of the work." (S014)
- Sources: S014, S079, S080, S088 (4).

## 6. Independent, asynchronous input (nominal group)
Collect each person's view in writing before the group talks, then spend meeting time only on the disagreements.
- Use: hiring, strategy calls, any group decision where the boss's view leaks.
- Score: guidance: if people have seen the boss's view before writing theirs, the input is contaminated.
- Quote: "The key is asynchronous work — asking for individual input before the discussion starts." (S068)
- Sources: S015, S067, S068, S087, S089, S096 (6).

## 7. Omission and commission bias, plus the stack against quitting
Staying feels like no decision, switching feels like an act, so regret falls on switching only. Stacked on top: loss aversion, sunk cost, endowment, status quo, identity.
- Use: any "stay or switch" call, firing, leaving a job or a market.
- Score: compare the odds, not the fear: "is 50% greater than zero?" (S080).
- Quote: "all these things really are stacked up against us ever stopping anything" (S079)
- Sources: S007, S021, S079, S080, S088, S091, S096 (7).

## 8. Knowledge tracking and the evidentiary record
Write down what you knew, what you could have known, and what revealed itself later. Better, build the record beforehand as pre-work so you can search your own decisions.
- Use: reviews, forecasts, investment memos.
- Quote: "We want to create a way to Google our decision-making." (S071)
- Sources: S071, S074, S076, S077 (4).

## 9. Impact, optionality and the time-accuracy trade-off
How bad the bad outcome is in the long run, and how reversible. Go fast when impact is low and you can exit; slow down when the reverse. The happiness test: will I care in a week, a month, a year?
- Use: setting how long to spend on a decision.
- Score: low impact plus high optionality means decide quickly.
- Quote: "The more you can reduce impact, the more quickly you can go." (S074)
- Sources: S074, S076, S077, S088, S090 (5).

## 10. Sort, then pick (only-option test)
Decisions are thresholding problems: sort options above and below a bar, then pick among the good ones freely. An option stays in the bucket if you would be happy had it been the only choice.
- Quote: "when a decision is really hard, that actually means it’s easy." (S088)
- Sources: S076, S077, S088 (3).

## 11. Decision stacking (date before you marry)
Run something small, reversible and informative before the large, illiquid choice: rent before buying, a beta before a launch, a pop-up before a lease.
- Quote: "I can think about how I can kind of date before I marry" (S074)
- Sources: S074, S076, S077 (3).

## 12. Premortem and backcasting (time travelling)
Imagine the goal reached, or the plan failed, and ask how you got there. Working backwards makes luck visible, which forward planning hides. Positive visualisation alone lowers the odds of finishing.
- Quote: "when you work backwards you naturally start to see the luck" (S090)
- Sources: S071, S089, S090, S096 (4).

## 13. Base rate first, then the causal reason to deviate
Start from what usually happens; move off it only for a named cause that has changed. Pair with a point estimate and lower and upper bounds.
- Quote: "start with the base rate. And then you better have a damn good reason that you’re deviating from it." (S093)
- Sources: S076, S087, S091, S093 (4).

## 14. Interrogating data: out of how many?
A true statistic still misleads without its denominator, comparison group and sample. Description is not explanation.
- Quote: "I need to know what the absolute risk is before I compare the relative risk." (S093)
- Sources: S032, S040, S093 (3).

## 15. Four levels of learning from outcomes
Level 1 blames losses on luck and credits wins to skill. Level 2 checks losses for both. Level 3 checks all four quadrants. Level 4 keeps digging after a win and a good decision. She credits the host for the levels.
- Quote: "we want to be thinking up-down orthogonal in all these directions" (S090)
- Sources: S090, S096, S074 (3).

## 16. Quitting coach
Someone who did not make the original decision, sees it from outside, and has explicit permission to say the hard thing.
- Quote: "get yourself a quitting coach because you’re in it." (S091)
- Sources: S080, S091 (2).

## 17. Decision group charter
Three agreements: accuracy over being right, tolerance for dissent, accountability to each other; two to disagree and one to referee.
- Quote: “You want two to disagree and one to referee.” (S101)
- Sources: S101, S067 (2).

## 18. Shadow book of near misses
Keep the near misses, plus a sample of clear misses, so a hidden bias toward no shows up in the aggregate.
- Quote: “let’s put things into three categories: clear misses, near misses, and hits.” (S096)
- Sources: S096 (1).

## Books

### Books: Annie Duke

Thesis in my words; no book text is reproduced.

#### Thinking in Bets (2018)
Decisions are bets on an uncertain future. Luck and hidden information break the link between outcome quality and decision quality, so judging by results ("resulting") teaches the wrong lessons. Frameworks: resulting, beliefs as bets, the three P's, premortem and backcasting, the decision group charter. Taught in S064 (a review, paraphrase), S067, S071, S076, S087, S090, S096, S101. Later she said it was too one-sided: it did not make clear that resulting cuts both ways (S071).

#### How to Decide (2020)
The "how" for the front half of a decision: imperfect information and beliefs, knowledge tracking, point forecasts with bounds, the happiness test, sorting and picking, the only-option test, decision stacking, impact and optionality. A workbook of tools. Taught in S071, S074, S076, S077, S088.

#### Quit: The Power of Knowing When to Walk Away (2022)
Winners quit often; adults quit too late. Biases stack against stopping: sunk cost, status quo, loss aversion, endowment, omission bias, identity. Tools: kill criteria with a state and a date, quitting coach, monkeys and pedestals, would-you-start-this-today. Taught in S025, S079, S080, S081, S082, S091.

#### Decide to Play Great Poker (poker book)
Named as her poker book on how to think through a hand, then use a solver (S080). No further detail in the sources.

#### Her next book (untitled in S093, 2026)
A checklist of questions to ask of any data, with no equations: out of how many, compared to what, who selected the sample. Not finished at the time of S093 (2026-01-08).

#### Major essays and series
Her Substack runs essays on bias, quitting and data: S025 (Why Quitting is Underrated), S014 (Monkeys and the Reverse Salient), S015 (Netflix and Qwikster), S032 (It's Not All Relative).
