# Frameworks: Bill Gurley

20 frameworks, most used first. Built from 109 sources (1996 to 2026): his Above the Crowd essays, long interviews and YouTube talks (Tim Ferriss, All-In 2022 to 2026, Invest Like the Best). Scoring guidance is labeled as guidance, drawn from his own thresholds.

## 1. The ten factors for a digital marketplace
- **What it is:** his checklist for whether a marketplace can win. Factors: (1) new experience vs the status quo, (2) economic advantage vs the status quo, (3) room for technology to add value, (4) high fragmentation, (5) low friction for suppliers to sign up, (6) market size, (7) ability to expand the market, (8) frequency of use, (9) being in the payment flow, (10) network effects.
- **When to use:** any marketplace idea, including a food or delivery one.
- **Scoring (guidance):** he says 7 or 8 of 10 beats 3 or 4 by a wide margin; size alone cannot rescue a low score.
- **Quote:** "a 7 or 8 out of 10 would imply that your opportunity of success is much, much higher than if you only match 3 or 4 out of 10." (S056)
- **Also:** "supplier aggregation is the easy part. Aggregating demand is much harder and more critical." (S056)
- **Sources:** S056, S055, S141, S129, S133.

## 2. Rake as a share of GMS, and the low-rake strategy
- **What it is:** the take rate is the commission as a percent of gross merchandise sales. A high rake becomes part of the landed price, so a platform that wants to be the definitive place to transact keeps it modest. Benchmarks he used: eBay just under 10%, Amazon marketplace 6 to 15%, affiliates 4 to 15% (about 10% on average), Airbnb about 11%, ride sharing about 20%, drivers keeping over 75% to 80%.
- **Steps:** start low for broad supplier adoption, prove the platform, then let suppliers who want more volume pay more on an opt-in basis (the Booking.com and AdWords pattern).
- **Scoring (guidance):** compare your rake with what your suppliers already pay for distribution; above the 10 to 15% band needs a reason, and count the effective rake (rake plus discounts the supplier funds).
- **Quote:** "A sustainable platform or marketplace is one where the value of being in the network clearly outshines the transactional costs charged for being in the network." (S057)
- **Sources:** S057, S023, S038, S043, S055, S099.

## 3. Price at what you should, not what you can extract
- **What it is:** the companion rule. A high margin invites a competitor, and total profit is margin times turnover. He cites oDesk cutting from 30% to 10% and winning, and Taobao beating eBay with free listing.
- **Quote:** "There is a big difference between what you can extract versus what you should extract. Water runs downhill." (S057)
- **Sources:** S057, S055, S099.

## 4. Revenue quality: the 10X Revenue Club scorecard
- **What it is:** ten tests of revenue: competitive advantage, network effects, visibility, lock-in, gross margin, marginal profitability, customer concentration, partner dependency, organic demand vs marketing spend, growth. Later additions: capex intensity, cash flow vs earnings, optionality.
- **Use:** never apply a flat revenue multiple. Compare gross margin on the incremental dollar and whether earnings turn into cash.
- **Scoring (guidance):** heavy marketing spend and a partner that can change its algorithm pull the grade down; many small price-taking customers and churn of 5% a year or less pull it up.
- **Quote:** "You will be hard pressed to find a company with a heavy marketing spend with a high price/revenue multiple." (S058)
- **Sources:** S058, S030, S059, S065, S142.

## 5. Selling dollars for eighty cents, and contra-revenue
- **What it is:** revenue is easy to buy below cost. Strip out coupons, discounts and subsidies (contra-revenue), use net revenue for a marketplace, and move variable costs above the margin line to see true contribution margin.
- **Quote:** "you can grow revenue as big as you want if you give away something below cost." (S142)
- **Quote:** "You should not pay a net revenue multiple for a gross revenue disclosure." (S053)
- **Sources:** S134, S058, S053, S044, S142.

## 6. LTV is a tool, not a strategy
- **What it is:** lifetime value (cash flow, not revenue, discounted and adjusted for churn) is for marketing to compare channels. As a strategy it breaks: the variables pull against each other, bought customers underperform organic ones, and the spending never stops.
- **Steps:** compute SAC on purchased customers only; let finance own the number; compare acquisition spend with an affiliate fee of 5 to 10% of sales, not 30 to 50%.
- **Quote:** "You can’t win a fight with a measuring tape." (S045)
- **Sources:** S045, S037, S058, S080, S127.

## 7. Conversion before acquisition
- **What it is:** treat acquisition as buying fuel and conversion as fuel efficiency. Build a conversion matrix by landing page, source and campaign. Twice the conversion lets you spend twice as much for the same outcome.
- **Quote:** "Think about customer acquisition as buying fuel, and customer optimization as measuring the fuel efficiency of your engine." (S037)
- **Sources:** S037, S127, S080, S088.

## 8. Network effects, increasing returns, and tipping
- **What it is:** from Brian Arthur. In some markets the leader gets locked in and wins or takes most. Test: can the marketplace give customer n+1000 a better experience than customer n? Many claimed network effects are only scale economies.
- **Quote:** "Network effects are rare but golden. If you don’t have one try to find one; if you do have one, try to enhance it." (S056)
- **Also (2025):** the 2,000th customer should have a way better experience than the thousandth; design that in, for example by letting one customer's learning improve the product for all. "that 2,000th customer should have a way better experience than the thousandth." (S146)
- **Sources:** S056, S049, S058, S059, S098, S102, S105, S146, S148.

## 9. The liquidity flywheel
- **What it is:** lower price raises demand, more liquidity, higher driver utilization, then room for another price cut. Pick-up time, coverage density and utilization are the three drivers. Features like pooling need high base liquidity, which favors the leader.
- **Quote:** "To make a concept like UberPool work you need a really high amount of base liquidity in the system – tons of cars and tons of riders." (S039)
- **Sources:** S039, S049, S038, S043.

## 10. Supply and demand curves in a marketplace (surge)
- **What it is:** when demand outstrips supply, a higher price does two jobs: it brings supply on and it cuts demand for a short time. The real alternative to dynamic pricing is empty screens. Be transparent, test elasticity first.
- **Quote:** "the only real alternative to dynamic pricing is a ton of customers staring at screens that read “No Cars Available.”" (S038)
- **Sources:** S038, S043.

## 11. Follow the money flows
- **What it is:** before predicting disruption or backing a company, trace who pays whom and what the incumbent's economics reward. Used on TV affiliate fees, Netflix pricing and healthcare.
- **Quote:** "it is critically important to understand where the money flows (most people don’t)." (S022)
- **Sources:** S022, S026, S135, S042, S018.

## 12. Business model beats product; can someone do it for free?
- **What it is:** compare business models before products. A rival that defends a profit pool elsewhere can price at zero. Ask the one question.
- **Quote:** "The key question for anyone in business is, “Can someone do what you do for free?”. If the answer is “yes” you have a problem." (S013)
- **Sources:** S013, S017, S020, S025, S054.

## 13. Regulatory capture
- **What it is:** from Stigler. Regulation is acquired by the industry and run for its benefit, through money, time around people and revolving doors. Test a law by its outcome, not its stated goal.
- **Quote:** "I like to say regulation is the friend of the incumbent." (S136)
- **Also:** the incumbents write the rules and lock themselves in; Boeing and the FAA, and TEPCO and the Japanese regulator, "they'd almost become friends." (S147)
- **Sources:** S136, S130, S135, S101, S059, S148, S149, S147.

## 14. Risk bubble and the sawtooth
- **What it is:** a bubble is measured by capital intensity, not peak price. Huge rounds push proof of the model into the future. Risk builds slowly, then drops sharply. A real technology wave brings bubble behavior (Perez), so a bubble call is not an attack on the technology.
- **Quote:** "People talk about boom, bust, and cycles, but this is set up more like a sawtooth. So risk on happens very slowly" (S059)
- **Also (2022):** learned from Howard Marks: venture has "low barriers to entry but yeah very high barriers to exit" (S154), so the cycle is built in. Risk-on is reflexive and slow; risk-off is abrupt. "it doesn't happen like a sine curve which is what we all imagine when we think of a cyclical business it's more like a sawtooth" (S154). Seven to 15 years between resets; the mental models only reach back three to five (S148).
- **Sources:** S053, S065, S059, S060, S137, S052, S154, S148, S149.

## 15. Dirty terms and the clean down round
- **What it is:** ratchets, PIK dividends, vetoes and preferences are a time bomb on the cap table. Ranking: clean down round, then get cash-flow positive, ahead of a dirty round at a high headline price.
- **Quote:** "Taking a terms-laden deal is like starting the clock on a time bomb." (S044)
- **Sources:** S044, S053, S065, S141.

## 16. Open source as defensive strategy
- **What it is:** a challenger donates technology to commoditize the leader's lock-in. Open source is more often a defensive weapon than an offensive one.
- **Quote:** "using open source as a defensive tool instead of an offensive tool is one of the most sophisticated corporate strategies a company can possibly do." (S060)
- **Also:** "I call it defensive corporate strategy." (S148) Examples he names: Android, Open Compute, Kubernetes.
- **Sources:** S083, S059, S060, S065, S081, S148, S149.

## 17. Time as the cost of capital
- **What it is:** a venture exit that slips from year 10 to year 15 must be worth far more for the same return, because the cost of capital compounds and employee equity dilutes 3 to 6% a year. His arithmetic: 15% cost plus 5% dilution means $100 must become about $250 after a five-year delay.
- **When to use:** a company told it can stay private longer, or a fund holding past the lock-up.
- **Scoring (guidance):** ask what the asset must be worth at the later date to earn the same return; if the answer is implausible, the delay is a cost, not a gift.
- **Quote:** "you have the cost of capital, the IRRa that just eats away." (S146)
- **Sources:** 1 (S146); the lock-up distribution habit (three to six quarters) is in S154.

## 18. Gavage-tube funding and the game on the field
- **What it is:** when investors push 100 to 300 million at any exciting company, founders are forced to swing for the fences, because a rival with that money will out-hire you. Play the game as it is while asking where it is going. Overfunding means seven initiatives, not two.
- **When to use:** a rival raised big; a founder asks whether to take the money.
- **Scoring (guidance):** count the initiatives the money would add and whether revenue survives a cut to break even.
- **Quote:** "they are forced to play the game on the field." (S146)
- **Sources:** 1 (S146), with the same logic in S053 (Fab) and S044.

## 19. Consumer-surplus business and negative unit economics
- **What it is:** a business where negative unit economics drive growth and customers win through subsidized rides, food or content while investors and employees lose. It works only if you can rein it back in, and usually needs an effective monopoly.
- **When to use:** any delivery or marketplace plan that buys orders with discounts.
- **Scoring (guidance):** can the subsidy stop without the volume leaving? If a funded rival can match it, assume the profit is competed away.
- **Quote:** "it all depends on whether you can rein it back in or not" (S154)
- **Sources:** 2 (S154, S146), related S053, S058.

## 20. Searchers and blockers, root cause first
- **What it is:** after a catastrophe some people investigate and others obstruct. Separate the technical failure from the institutional one, then run CAPA: detect, investigate, confirm root cause, correct, prevent. Do not attack the blockers; move around them.
- **When to use:** an incident, outage or dispute where blame is replacing diagnosis.
- **Quote:** "you can't skip root cause." (S147)
- **Sources:** 1 (S147), the 2026 All-In talk. A late interest, outside marketplace work.

## Books

### Books: Bill Gurley

One book of his own, plus the essays that carry his marketplace thinking. Summaries are in my own words. Built from 104 sources (1996 to 2026).

#### Runnin' Down a Dream (2025)
- **Core thesis:** a career guide, not a venture memoir. Chase curiosity, hone your craft by continuous learning, find mentors, embrace your peers. The test of the right lane is whether you study it at night without being told.
- **Key frameworks:** the 30-year test, the mentor two rungs up, the fanboy journal (from Danny Meyer), the regret test.
- **Taught in sources:** S060, S137, S140, S068, S001. It is off-topic for marketplace decisions and rarely needed.

#### Essays that work as his books on marketplaces
- **A Rake Too Far (2013, S057):** low rake as strategy, opt-in paid placement, the effective rake.
- **All Markets Are Not Created Equal (2012, S056):** the ten factors for a digital marketplace.
- **Money Out of Nowhere (2019, S055):** why marketplaces unlock wealth, seven benefits, vertical over horizontal.
- **The Keys to the 10X Revenue Club (2011, S058):** revenue quality.
- **The Dangerous Seduction of the LTV Formula (2012, S045):** LTV as a tool, not a strategy.
- **Investors Beware (2015, S053) and On the Road to Recap (2016, S044):** the risk bubble, dirty terms, down rounds.
- **2,851 Miles (2023 talk, S136):** regulatory capture.

#### Books he recommends (not his own)
Competitive Strategy (Porter), Increasing Returns and the Two Worlds of Business (Arthur), The Innovator's Dilemma and Crossing the Chasm, Complexity (Waldrop), The Rational Optimist and How Innovation Works (Ridley), Catastrophic Care (Goldhill), Startup (Kaplan), Technological Revolutions and Financial Capital (Perez). Sources: S059, S060, S061, S135, S058.
