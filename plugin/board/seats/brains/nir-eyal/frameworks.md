# Frameworks: Nir Eyal

Built from 240 of his essays on nirandfar.com (2011 to 2025), standard depth. Hooked-era (2012 to 2016) frameworks come first in each pair, then the Indistractable-era limits (2016 to 2025). Scoring lines are guidance for the council, not his thresholds, unless a number is quoted. Co-authored and guest material is credited to the other authors in the notes and is not used here as his.

## 1. The Hooked Model
Trigger, Action, Variable Reward, Investment. A loop that connects a user's problem to a habit-forming product. The action is the simplest behavior done in anticipation of relief. The investment (time, data, effort, social capital, money) makes the next pass better, so unlike a sales funnel it has no endpoint. Use it to design or to diagnose a product, and also, turned around, to take a bad habit apart (S116, S117).
- Score (guidance): one point per phase that is present and named in the user's own words; a missing internal trigger or investment caps the habit.
- Quote: "a trigger to begin using the product, an action to satisfy the trigger, a variable reward for the action, and some type of investment that, ultimately, makes the product more valuable to the user" (S057)
- Quote: "The Hooked Model is a design pattern to connect a user’s problem to a habit-forming product." (S131)
- Sources: S057, S251, S283, S285, S290, S312, S321, S327, S131, S117, S226, S076 (12).

## 2. Internal versus external triggers
External triggers are in the environment (a ring, an ad, a badge). Internal triggers are thoughts or feelings tied to the product that fire on their own. Hooked-era: the goal is to move users from external to internal. Indistractable-era: the same split is the first pillar of self-control, because the internal trigger is the discomfort behind the urge.
- Score (guidance): ask what feeling fires the use. If the answer is a notification only, there is no habit yet.
- Quote: "Internal triggers manifest automatically in the mind and creating them is the brass ring of consumer technology." (S055)
- Quote: "An “internal trigger” is an uncomfortable emotion we seek to escape and it’s the root of our tendency to go off track." (S108)
- Sources: S055, S057, S108, S240, S238, S250, S212 (7+).

## 3. The Regret Test
Ask whether people, knowing everything the designer knows, would still do the intended behavior, or would regret it. Test with a sample of users (five can be enough) and repeat it as a check-in, not only on a feature. Fail it and the technique is coercion, not persuasion. This is the seat's main instrument.
- Score (guidance): count the share of sampled users who say they regret the behavior. Any real share is a stop-and-redesign signal; he sets a pass bar but gives no number, so the council must set its own.
- Quote: "If people knew everything the product designer knows, would they still execute the intended behavior? Are they likely to regret doing this?" (S042)
- Quote: "Getting people to do something they didn’t want to do is no longer persuasion — it’s coercion." (S042)
- Sources: S042, S253 (2); echoed in S301.

## 4. The Manipulation Matrix
Two questions asked before any code: will I use the product myself, and will it materially improve users' lives? Four boxes. Facilitator (yes, yes) has met its moral duty. Peddler (would not use, believes it helps) has low odds because of the empathy gap. Entertainer (would use, cannot claim it improves lives) lives on hits. Dealer (neither) is exploitation. It answers should I, not can I.
- Score (guidance): place the founder honestly. Squirming, or needing a preamble starting "If I were a...", means fail.
- Quote: "Creating a product that the designer does not believe improves the user’s life and which the maker would not use is exploitation." (S003)
- Sources: S003, S301 (2). Note: the 2012 text puts ultimate responsibility for overuse on the user; the later Use and Abuse Policy softens that.

## 5. Use and Abuse Policy
Companies set triggers for intervention and upper limits, find users who are truly addicted (no longer in control, want to stop, cannot), and reach out with a simple offer of help, as a concerned friend would. If the user says no, leave them alone. Do not ban features for everyone.
- Score (guidance): can the company name its heavy-use threshold and what it says to a user over it? No policy plus the data to build one is, in his words, exploitation.
- Quote: "Companies have an obligation to establish what I call a, “Use and Abuse Policy,”" (S015)
- Quote: "it is unethical to accept patronage from someone a company knows wants to stop using your product but can’t." (S019)
- Quote: "If the user says no, that’s fine." (S254)
- Sources: S015, S019, S113, S139, S254 (5).

## 6. The Indistractable Model (four pillars)
Master internal triggers; make time for traction; hack back external triggers; block out distraction with pacts. Do not skip ahead to pacts. Use the model first to diagnose the problem, then pick the tool.
- Score (guidance): name which of the four is the cause before recommending any app or rule.
- Quote: "Don’t skip the first three steps." (S008)
- Quote: "Use it first to diagnose the problem, then search for the solution." (S227)
- Sources: S008, S212, S171, S227, S220, S223 (6).

## 7. Traction versus distraction
Traction is any action done with intent, from the Latin for to pull. Distraction pulls you from what you planned. The opposite of distraction is traction, not focus. You cannot call something a distraction unless you know what it distracts you from. Scrolling is fine if planned.
- Score (guidance): compare what was planned with what happened, not tasks ticked.
- Quote: "If you find yourself doing what you planned, that’s traction. Anything else is a distraction." (S032)
- Quote: "There’s nothing wrong with scrolling through your Facebook feed, watching YouTube videos or playing a video game, as long as that’s what you intend to do." (S032)
- Sources: S032, S068, S069, S105, S246, S152 (6+).

## 8. The Habit Zone
Plot frequency of use against perceived utility. Habit forms where both are high enough for the behavior to become the default. Infrequent actions need high perceived utility.
- Score (guidance): estimate real use frequency per week; below daily-to-weekly with weak utility means no habit.
- Quote: "The Habit Zone,” as I call it, is where an action occurs with enough frequency and perceived utility for it to become the default behavior." (S049)
- Sources: S049 (1); related S305 (frequency tracks satisfaction).

## 9. Habit Testing (Identify, Codify, Modify)
Find your habitual users, codify the path they share (the habit path), then push new users down it. Set a realistic expected frequency; if fewer than about 5 percent of users reach it, rework the vision. Use cohorts. He admits the hypothesis step can confuse correlation with causation.
- Score (guidance): share of users meeting the expected frequency against his 5 percent line.
- Quote: "if at least 5% of your users don’t find your product valuable enough to use as much as you predicted they should, you have a problem" (S054)
- Sources: S054, S006, S076 (3).

## 10. The habit-forming test (toothbrush test)
Is the product used once or twice a day, or does it relieve a recurring itch? About half the companies that ask him to make a product habit-forming fail the test, and that is fine. Rarely used products build habits around the product (content, community) instead.
- Score (guidance): honest answer to how often a user would need it with no reminder.
- Quote: "Not every business needs to be habit-forming." (S007)
- Quote: "Sorry, you don’t meet the test. Your product will never become a habit." (S191)
- Sources: S007, S076, S191, S312 (4).

## 11. Goods, necessities, distractions, addictions
A two-by-two: harmful or not, and can I stop or not. A distraction harms but can be stopped if you choose; an addiction harms and cannot be stopped. His chart is personal, drawn for himself and not for every reader.
- Score (guidance): place the product for the target user, not the average user. Most heavy use lands in distractions.
- Quote: "“Distractions” are defined as behaviors that harm us but that we can stop doing, if we choose." (S073)
- Sources: S073, S016, S113 (3).

## 12. The Q-tip Effect (solution becomes the problem)
A perceived solution turns into the source of the problem, moving use to abuse. Two kinds of makers: those who do not know their users (warn them) and those who know exactly (help them).
- Quote: "The Q-tip Effect arises when the solution becomes the source of the problem, eventually leading from use to abuse." (S019)
- Quote: "Like alcohol to a drunk, the solution becomes the problem." (S161)
- Sources: S019, S161 (2).

## 13. Stored value (the Investment phase)
Whenever users put data, content, reputation or effort into a service, it improves with use and the user is more likely to return. Lock-in comes from many small investments, not hardware.
- Score (guidance): list what the user has stored and whether leaving costs them something real.
- Quote: "Whenever users put data into a service, they invest in it and increase the likelihood of returning, a phenomenon I call “stored value”." (S321)
- Sources: S057, S271, S290, S321, S341 (5; S341 is co-authored with Sangeet Paul Choudary).

## 14. Finite versus infinite variability, and LIFO habits
Finite rewards (a single-player game, a film, a slot machine) become predictable and fade. Infinite ones (other people, user content, tribes) stay novel. Old behaviors win back: last in, first out. Habits give an edge, not a guarantee.
- Quote: "behaviors are LIFO — last in, first out." (S344)
- Quote: "Habits do not ensure perpetual users, but short of a disruptive change, they provide an opportunity to form a sustainable competitive advantage." (S344)
- Sources: S344, S310 (2).

## 15. Timeboxing and turning values into time
Values (attributes of the person you want to become) become blocks on a calendar. Success is doing what you planned, not finishing. Review weekly for 15 minutes. Triple your time estimate.
- Quote: "Keep in mind the only measure of success is not “how many boxes did I tick-off on my to-do list?,” but rather, “did I do what I intended to do without getting distracted.”" (S011)
- Sources: S011, S012, S013, S105, S107, S134, S135, S225, S246 (9+).

## 16. The ten-minute rule (surf the urge)
When the urge hits, give in only after ten minutes. Notice the sensation and ride it. Usually it passes.
- Quote: "If we still want to perform the action after ten minutes of urge surfing, we’re free to do it; but that’s rarely the case." (S009)
- Sources: S009, S162, S224, S134, S220 (5).

## 17. Pacts (effort, price, identity)
Precommitment removes a future choice. Effort pacts add friction, price pacts put money on the line, identity pacts tie to a self-image. Use only after the first three steps, for short bursts. Few take the bet; constant triggers defeat it.
- Quote: "A precommitment is a pledge that removes a future choice to overcome unwanted impulses." (S060)
- Quote: "pacts aren’t good for every goal and must be done after implementing the first three steps to becoming indistractable." (S208)
- Sources: S010, S060, S208, S212, S022, S141, S152 (7).

## 18. Minimum Enjoyable Action (MEA)
Shrink the behavior until it is so easy and pleasant it happens almost without effort. If you keep missing it, shrink it.
- Quote: "A MEA is a behavior, which is so easy and enjoyable, that we do it virtually without effort, and most importantly, we can do it consistently." (S145)
- Sources: S145, S218, S203, S129 (4). Note: S145 is co-written with Charles Wang.

## 19. Habit versus routine
A habit is an impulse to act with little or no thought, and not doing it feels bad. A routine is a series of actions frequently repeated and takes effort. Hard work such as writing stays a routine; building a habit for it is the wrong tool.
- Quote: "A habit is an impulse to do a behavior with little or no conscious thought." (S024)
- Quote: "A habit is a behavior that requires no conscious thought, and that is certainly not writing." (S134)
- Sources: S024, S134, S140, S132, S225 (5).

## 20. Reactance and wanna versus hafta
People resist being told what to do. Products fail when they feel like haftas. Reaffirm freedom, give options, reframe "have to" as "get to".
- Quote: "People must want to use the service, not feel they have to." (S028)
- Quote: "The psychological phenomenon of reactance tells us people tend to resist doing things they feel coerced into completing." (S142)
- Sources: S028, S061, S142, S156, S209 (5).

## 21. Viral loop versus viral oops, and dark patterns
A viral loop is a normal-use invite where both sides gain. A viral oops relies on user error and the user blames themselves. A roach motel makes sign-up easy and cancel hard.
- Quote: "A viral oops not only deceives the user, it fools the developer." (S334)
- Quote: "make it as easy to cancel a subscription as it is to start one" (S253)
- Sources: S303, S334, S253 (3).

## 22. The California Roll Rule
New things need a familiar gateway. Ask what the product's California Roll is, then move users to sashimi.
- Quote: "people don’t want something truly new, they want the familiar done differently." (S277)
- Sources: S277, S273 (2).

## 23. Kano model (delighters, linear, hygienic)
Customers can ask for linear needs but cannot ask for delighters. Never break a hygienic feature. A product can win with flaws if delighters make up for them.
- Quote: "Products can win markets by ensuring their “delighter” features compensate for flaws." (S275)
- Sources: S275 (1).

## 24. GEM: growth, engagement, monetization
Three linked questions, each with a metric. Never optimize one alone. Habit is one of five moats.
- Quote: "Growth, engagement, and monetization are interlinked, and each is insufficient on its own." (S267)
- Sources: S267 (1); related S007 (monetization follows engagement).

## 25. Psychological nutrients (autonomy, competence, relatedness)
Distractions satisfy deficiencies. A person, especially a child, who lacks these three seeks them in devices. Set limits with them, not for them.
- Quote: "If they don’t get sufficient amounts of these nutrients, they seek them in distractions, overindulging in social media and video games." (S133)
- Sources: S040, S133, S149 (3). The theory is Ryan and Deci's; Eyal applies it.

## Books

### Books: Nir Eyal

Built from 240 of his essays, 2012 to 2025. No book text is reproduced. Core ideas come from the essays that preview each book.

#### Hooked: How to Build Habit-Forming Products (2014)
- Idea: a four-phase loop (trigger, action, variable reward, investment) that ties a user's problem to a product.
- Previewed in the 2012 essays S057, S055, S054, S049, S344 and the ethics essay S003.
- In balance: the book arrives with a Manipulation Matrix and the later Regret Test. He says the book was meant to teach healthy habits (S158), and that he is "not so sure" after 2018.
- Where he is weak: S158 admits uncertainty, and the book leans on a designer's view of user benefit.

#### Indistractable: How to Control Your Attention and Choose Your Life (2019)
- Idea: master internal triggers, make time for traction, hack back external triggers, prevent distraction with pacts.
- Previewed in S008, S032, S009, S010, S011, S212, S233 and the 2020 to 2025 essays.
- In balance: shifts blame from technology to unmet discomfort and the plan; keeps the tech-maker duty to avoid exploitation.
- Where he is weak: advice assumes control over one's calendar; the claim that willpower is not limited rests on a contested literature (S065, S066).

#### The essays and series at nirandfar.com
- Hooked-era series, 2011 to 2016 (the Hooked posts, Habit Testing, the Habit Zone, ethics pieces).
- Indistractable-era series, 2016 to 2025 (timeboxing, pacts, kids and tech, work culture, dark patterns, regulation).
- Use newer essays when they disagree with older ones.
