# Frameworks: Robert Cialdini

Built from 16 sources (quick depth, about half the usual entries). Scoring lines are guidance inferred from his findings, not his thresholds.

## 1. Seven principles of influence
Reciprocation, liking, social proof, authority, scarcity, commitment and consistency, unity. Found by going undercover in sales, recruiting and fundraising training. Use when you need to know which lever fits a request. Score: count how many are really present in the facts; a case with none needs a better offer, not a trick.
"Have you ever seen these lists of behavioral-science biases? There’re like 100 of them. Well, no, there are just seven. We can handle seven." (S003) Sources: S003, S007, S008, S010, S020, S029 (six in the older ones: S018, S019).

## 2. Pre-suasion
Persuasion puts motivators into the message; pre-suasion directs attention to one of them before it. Steps: pick your best true strength, then bring an idea or image consistent with it to mind first. Score: does the first thing the customer sees point to the strength you will claim?
"It is what you say immediately before you deliver your message that leverages your success tremendously." (S015) Sources: S005, S012, S015, S025, S028.

## 3. Real versus counterfeit (influence versus manipulation)
Point to a principle where it exists; do not invent it. Use for any promotion or copy review. Score: if you removed the claim, would the facts still support it? If no, stop.
"the big difference is whether the principles of influence are employed by pointing to them where they naturally exist versus manufacturing or counterfeiting them." (S003) Sources: S003, S005, S010, S014, S018, S019.

## 4. Reciprocation done right
Go first, make it personal, make it unexpected. Mints: one mint raised tips, two raised them more, and a personalized extra raised them most (S012: about 3, 14 and 21 percent). Score: gift given before the ask, fitted to the person, not announced as a deal.
"be the first to give and to ensure that what you give is personalized and unexpected." (S020) Sources: S007, S008, S010, S012, S014, S020, S025.

## 5. Unity
Shared identity (one of us) is stronger than similarity. Bring an existing identity to mind; do not invent one. Score: is there a real "we" (same neighborhood, same trade)? Name it.
"Stephen is like us, versus Stephen is one of us. If I can say that, all influence barriers come down for you inside that group." (S003) Sources: S003, S005, S008, S011, S014, S026.

## 6. Commitment and consistency
Ask for an active, public, voluntary step, then pause. Restaurant no-shows fell after the script changed to ask for a spoken answer. Score: did the person say yes out loud or in writing, freely?
"People want to be consistent with what they have already committed themselves to, especially in public, by what they have said or done previously." (S007) Sources: S007, S008, S014, S018, S019, S020.

## 7. Social proof from comparable others
People copy people like them; hotel guests of "this very room" beat "most guests". Point to the truth, never report that a bad behavior is common (boomerang). Score: is the proof from similar people, and is it true?
"Well, that makes their behavior even more diagnostic of what we should do." (S005) Sources: S005, S014, S018, S020, S026.

## 8. Scarcity as loss
Losing is about twice as motivating as gaining. Limited quantity beats limited time because it adds competition. Exclusive information raises the effect. Score: is the item truly dwindling or unique?
"The prospects of losing something of value are twice as potent in motivating action than the prospects of gaining that very same resource." (S005) Sources: S005, S008, S014, S018, S019, S020.

## 9. Authority shown first
Real expertise, put ahead of the pitch and by someone else when possible. Stroke therapists with diplomas on the wall gained compliance. Two authorities are not a dilution. Score: is the credential relevant to this domain, and is the source even-handed?
"My view is that it should go first." (S008) Sources: S003, S007, S008, S014, S019, S020, S026.

## 10. Rejection then retreat (door in the face)
A large request refused, then a smaller one, doubles acceptance because your concession draws one back. Score: only valid if the first ask is reasonable and the second is the real one.
"Our concession powerfully stimulated return concessions." (S018) Sources: S007, S010, S018.

## 11. Contrast principle
After a large number, the next price looks small; start at a large anchor, not an inflated fake price. Score: is the anchor real and stated before your price?
"which made $300 seem like a pittance" (S011) Sources: S010, S011, S015.

## 12. Ask for advice, not opinion
One word turns a critic into a partner. Use when you want buy-in on a plan.
"The wrong thing to do is to ask for their opinion because when you ask for opinion you get a critic." (S005) Sources: S005, S007, S008, S014, S026, S028.

## 13. Precise numbers and trend
A specific number signals homework; a rising trend turns a small share into a lever.
"Round numbers sound like they can’t pull them out someplace lower." (S026) Sources: S007, S026, S028.

## 14. Combine the principles
Principles work in combination.
"they should be applied in combination to compound their impact." (S019) Sources: S019, S011, S014.

## Books

### Books: Robert Cialdini

Built from 16 sources (quick depth). Summaries are in my words, not the books' text.

#### Influence: The Psychology of Persuasion (1984; Science and Practice 4th ed. 2001; New and Expanded 2021)
Thesis: a few mental shortcuts (six, then seven with unity) drive most yes answers, and anyone can learn to use them honestly or resist them. Frameworks: the seven principles, reciprocal concessions, the boomerang of social proof, active-public-voluntary commitment. Written for consumers first; business adopted it, and it sold slowly for about three years before it took off (S029). The 2021 edition adds unity, the internet and an ethics emphasis (S003, S008). Taught in S003, S007, S008, S010, S018, S019, S020, S026, S029.

#### Pre-Suasion: A Revolutionary Way to Influence and Persuade (2016)
Thesis: what you do in the moment before the message sets the frame for how the message lands. Frameworks: privileged moments, attention as importance, matching cues to the goal, self-concept priming (asking if someone is helpful), mystery openers. About a third is endnotes (S029); he waited about 30 years for an idea big enough (S025). Taught in S005, S012, S015, S025, S028, S029.

#### Articles
"Harnessing the Science of Persuasion" (HBR, 2001; S019) and "The Science of Persuasion" (Scientific American, 2001; S018) condense the six principles for managers. No other books of his are discussed in the sources. He recommends Give and Take by Adam Grant (S014, S026) and Happy Money (S029).
