# Stories: Peter Thiel

Built from 25 sources. Stories from S011 are told in a student's class notes, not his own transcript. All figures are as he or the notes gave them.

1. **PayPal found a first market.** About 20,000 eBay power sellers looked like poor customers, yet PayPal reached 25 to 30 percent penetration in two or three months, then widened. Lesson: a tiny market is the beachhead. (S044, S050)
2. **Facebook at Harvard.** About 10,000 people, 60 percent share in ten days; business schools called it too small to matter. Lesson: start small, own it. (S044, S050)
3. **PayPal's pivot to e-mail.** Money beaming between Palm Pilots was voted one of the ten worst business ideas of 1999; linking money to e-mail was the big, easy secret. Only about 3 million merchants took cards but 150 to 200 million people had e-mail. (S003, S011, S056)
4. **PayPal against X.com.** Four blocks apart, feature for feature, 90 to 100 hour weeks, then a 50-50 merger in March 2000. Lesson: choose enemies well, or merge. (S011)
5. **The law firm exit.** After seven months and three days he left a big New York firm. A colleague said he had not known one could escape from Alcatraz. Lesson: people tie identity to winning a competition and lose sight of what is valuable. (S001, S044, S050, S052)
6. **The clerkship he did not get.** Winning it would have meant more competition and no PayPal. Lesson: the last prize can be the wrong one. (S011)
7. **Fraud at PayPal.** Twenty investigators were overwhelmed; a human-plus-computer system fixed it and became Palantir's model. Fraud also scared off the big banks. (S003)
8. **The eBay stand-off.** About 75 percent of PayPal volume sat on eBay; five talks over two years ended after 30 people in PayPal t-shirts showed up at eBay's convention. Lesson: when the buyer is the only buyer, price is about leverage. (S022)
9. **October 2000 at PayPal.** About $10 million a month burn and 4.5 months of cash; he returned as interim CEO and named David Sacks COO. (S011)
10. **Airlines against Google.** A hundred years of cumulative US airline profit is about zero; search is smaller yet worth more. Lesson: X and Y are independent. (S044, S045, S050)
11. **Disk drives in the 1980s and textile mills.** Huge gains for customers, nothing kept by the founders, because each was replaced in two years. Lesson: no last-mover position, no capture. (S044)
12. **Castro Street restaurants.** PayPal, with fewer staff, held more capital value than all of them together. Lesson: a big market is a hard way to make money. (S011)
13. **Solyndra and clean tech.** Every deck began with a vast market; a cylinder has less sun-facing surface than a flat panel, so ninth-grade geometry showed it inferior. Lesson: judge by substance. (S001, S011, S044)
14. **Tesla and SpaceX in 2008.** Few thought SpaceX, six years old, was worth backing; no one had invested in rockets for 40 years. Weak competition was the opening. (S011, S022)
15. **Yahoo's offer for Facebook.** A billion dollars in July 2006; Zuckerberg opened the meeting by calling it a formality. Lesson: a founder with a definite plan does not sell. (S051, book text read by a host)
16. **The iPod in 2001.** Analysts saw a feature for Mac users; Jobs planned the first of a new generation. A long plan looks plain from outside. (S051, book text read by a host)
17. **The sysadmin without backups.** Hired outside the network under a no-fire rule; an engineer had backed everything up in secret. Lesson: fire before it is obvious. (S011)
18. **Harvard Business School cohorts.** The largest group goes into the field that just peaked: 1989, 1999 to 2000, 2005 to 2007. Lesson: the crowd is late. (S003, S032, S050, S052)
19. **The Apollo and Manhattan comparison.** Empire State Building in 15 months; the World Trade Center rebuild was in its twelfth year. Lesson: we lost the ability to build. (S053)
20. **The 1967 book The American Challenge.** It forecast a four-day week and 13 weeks of holiday; it did not happen, which he uses to show how far growth fell short. (S047, S053)
